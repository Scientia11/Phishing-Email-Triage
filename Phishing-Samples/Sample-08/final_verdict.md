# Final Verdict

## Executive Summary

A comprehensive phishing investigation was conducted on an email claiming to originate from **Capital One Financial Corporation** with the subject **"Action Required: New Payment Temporary on Hold."** The investigation included analysis of the email headers, sender infrastructure, originating IP address, email content, embedded hyperlinks, and the attached HTML file.

Although the email attempted to impersonate Capital One by using familiar branding and legitimate Capital One footer hyperlinks, the technical evidence revealed that the message did **not** originate from Capital One's infrastructure. The sender used an unrelated domain (**xserver.com**), the email originated from a Japanese hosting provider (**KAGOYA JAPAN Inc.**), and the message lacked SPF, DKIM, and DMARC authentication.

The investigation also identified a malicious HTML attachment that was detected by multiple security vendors and classified as spyware during sandbox analysis. These findings provide strong evidence that the email was designed to trick recipients into opening a malicious attachment and compromising sensitive information.

# Investigation Summary

## 1. Subject Analysis

**Subject:**

> **Action Required: New Payment Temporary on Hold**

The subject attempts to create urgency by informing the recipient that a payment is on hold and requires immediate action. It also contains awkward grammar ("Temporary" instead of "Temporarily"), which is uncommon in professionally reviewed communications from major financial institutions.

**Finding:**

* Urgency-based social engineering.
* Financial-themed lure.
* Poor grammar.
* Encourages immediate user action.

---

## 2. Email Header Analysis

The header analysis identified several inconsistencies between the claimed sender and the underlying email infrastructure.

### Sender Information

**From:**

```text
winner@xserver.com
```

**Return-Path:**

```text
winner@xserver.com
```

The sender claims to represent **Capital One**, yet the email originates from **xserver.com**, a domain unrelated to Capital One. The generic mailbox name **winner** further reduces credibility, as legitimate banking notifications are typically sent from addresses such as *support@*, *alerts@*, or *no-reply@* on the organization's official domain.

---

### Message-ID

The Message-ID was generated by **xserver.com**, indicating that the email originated from Xserver's infrastructure rather than Capital One's enterprise email systems.

---

### Email Authentication

| Authentication | Result |
| -------------- | ------ |
| SPF            | None   |
| DKIM           | None   |
| DMARC          | None   |

The complete absence of SPF, DKIM, and DMARC is a significant anomaly. Financial institutions such as Capital One typically implement all three authentication mechanisms to prevent email spoofing and protect customers from phishing attacks.

---

## 3. Originating IP Investigation

The originating IP address:

```text
133.18.212.99
```

was investigated using WHOIS.

### WHOIS Findings

* **Location:** Osaka, Japan
* **Network:** Japan Network Information Center (JPNIC)
* **ASN:** AS24282 – KAGOYA JAPAN Inc.
* **Resolved Hostname:** `v133-18-212-99.vir.kagoya.net`

The infrastructure belongs to **KAGOYA JAPAN Inc.**, a legitimate Japanese hosting provider. However, there is no known relationship between KAGOYA and Capital One.

This indicates that the email was genuinely sent from Japanese hosting infrastructure but not from infrastructure associated with Capital One.

---

## 4. Email Content Analysis

The email claims that the recipient has received a new incoming payment and instructs them to download an attached HTML file to review the payment and verify account ownership.

Several phishing indicators were identified:

* Generic greeting ("Dear Valuable Customer").
* Financial incentive through an incoming payment.
* Request to verify account ownership.
* Instruction to open an unsolicited HTML attachment.
* Poor grammar.
* Limited branding.
* Artificial urgency regarding payment processing.

The email relies heavily on social engineering techniques designed to encourage recipients to interact with the attachment without independently verifying the request.

---

## 5. Footer URL Analysis

The footer contained several hyperlinks directing users to Capital One resources, including:

* Contact Us
* Help Prevent Fraud
* Privacy
* Capital One Mobile App

All extracted links pointed to the legitimate domain:

```text
click-notification.capitalone.com
```

which is a valid subdomain of **capitalone.com** used for email click tracking.

While these links are legitimate, they do **not** validate the email as authentic. Attackers frequently copy legitimate footers from genuine emails to increase the credibility of phishing messages.

# Final Assessment

**Classification:** **Confirmed Phishing Email**

**Confidence Level:** **High**

The investigation concludes with high confidence that this email is a phishing campaign impersonating **Capital One Financial Corporation**. Although the attacker incorporated legitimate Capital One footer links to make the message appear authentic, every critical technical indicator contradicts the claimed identity. The email originated from **xserver.com** using infrastructure hosted by **KAGOYA JAPAN Inc.** in Japan, rather than Capital One's own infrastructure, and lacked SPF, DKIM, and DMARC authentication.

The social engineering tactics—including a pending payment notification, a request to verify account ownership, and pressure to act—were designed to entice the recipient into opening the attached HTML file. Threat intelligence confirmed that the attachment is malicious: it was detected by **16 security vendors on VirusTotal**, classified as **spyware** by **Hybrid Analysis**, and mapped to **MITRE ATT&CK T1204.002 (User Execution: Malicious File)**.

This investigation also illustrates an important lesson for email triage: **legitimate branding and authentic-looking footer links should never be considered proof of legitimacy**. Attackers frequently reuse genuine logos, contact information, and official hyperlinks to build trust while delivering malicious payloads through other components of the email.

Based on the combined technical, behavioral, and threat intelligence evidence, the email should be treated as **malicious**, the attachment should **not** be opened outside of an isolated analysis environment, and the identified indicators of compromise should be added to organizational email filtering, endpoint detection, and threat intelligence systems.
